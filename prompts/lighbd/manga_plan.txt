VISUAL NARRATIVE GRAMMAR

Use Neil Cohn's Visual Narrative Grammar to organize the source's meaning:
- Establisher (E): orient the reader to participants and their relationship before the focal interaction develops.
- Initial (I): introduce the action or preparation leading into the focal event.
- Prolongation (L): extend an ongoing action or delay its culmination; this concerns presentation, not necessarily elapsed time.
- Peak (P): carry the sequence's central information, such as an action's culmination, interruption or decisive change.
- Release (R): show the aftermath or response relative to that Peak.

A canonical phase is (E) -> (I (L)) -> P -> (R), with parentheses marking optional roles. This is a reference pattern, not five required panels. Roles depend on context, not panel number or camera distance. Peak means informational focus, not an obligation to add spectacle or a joke.

Roles can belong to coherent groups of panels as well as individual panels. A small action sequence can therefore serve one role within a larger event. Distinguish the source events from the way their information is presented.

PLANNING PROCEDURE FOR THIS RECAP

1. Recover the selected passage's participants, starting state, actions, causes, key change, result and visible reactions. Resolve who does what to whom from the full context. Work inside the assigned beginning/end anchors; later CURRENT text is not recap material. Sentence count does not determine panel count.
2. Locate the Peak first, then its supporting Initial and source-established Release; consider Establishers and Prolongations afterward. Treat role assignments as hypotheses. Test a proposed omission: does it erase the central information, make the action abrupt, or remove an aftermath the source actually supplied? That information needs representation. A changed camera angle alone does not establish a new role.
3. Check which candidate beats form coherent event groups. Inspect adjacent two- or three-beat windows: keep each cause connected to its own action and result. Compress a secondary group as a unit when its internal detail is expendable; do not splice one event's setup onto another event's consequence.
4. Allocate 3 or 4 panels using the smallest readable sequence. For a completed action, try I-P-R, with setting visible alongside the Initial. Use E-I-P-R when separate orientation is necessary, or I-L-P-R when an intermediate step explains the mechanism. A complex central event may occupy two panels serving a grouped Peak. These are project planning options, not mandatory formulas. A fourth panel must contribute useful source information or a necessary spatial/causal cue.
5. Preserve the source's actual endpoint. If it ends during preparation or action, show supported moments without manufacturing a Peak or Release. For a static passage, show complementary views of the same state and make simultaneity clear. For several short linked events, retain their source order and causal chain instead of forcing them into one artificial climax. Each panel depicts one coherent instant; only combine setting and action when they can coexist at that instant.

INFERENCE AND OMISSION

Cohn and Kutas distinguish an unshown event that has an anticipatory visual cue from an unshown event with no such cue. A cue can prepare the reader to interpret the following result; a passive onlooker or generic reaction is not automatically informative.

For each omitted step, identify what the reader must infer and which retained visible facts support it. Put those facts in the panel descriptions: relevant object states, motion direction, contact, directed gaze or a specific source-supported reaction. Ask whether another materially different event would fit the same pictures just as well. If the causal connection becomes ambiguous, retain the missing action or replace a redundant panel with it. Do not invent a clue to justify an omission. A clue needs enough space in the panel to be readable.

Keep essential causes and outcomes; compress repeated travel, redundant views and intermediate motion when the surviving sequence conveys them. Preserve source dialogue when the words themselves carry the event. Otherwise, check whether the visible action remains understandable without explanatory balloons. Do not turn narration into invented speech to repair an unclear sequence.

CONTRASTING BEAT EXAMPLES

These illustrate selection and compression; apply the active cast and visibility policy when framing them.
- Source: someone oversleeps, rushes out, misses the bus and arrives late. Four beats preserve the chain: waking late -> rushing out -> missed departure -> late arrival. The last beat is the supplied consequence, not an added punchline.
- Source: someone carrying water steps on a toy, loses balance and splashes another person. Three beats can show the approaching foot/toy and cup -> the slip and spilling water -> the recipient wet. Keep the slip visible when jumping directly to a wet person would conceal the source's cause. Renaming the people or paraphrasing the accident does not change this decision.
- Source: a person waits at a closed gate with a key in hand; nothing opens yet. Three complementary views may show the waiting figure, the held key and the still-closed gate at the same time. An unlocked gate, entry or relieved reaction would invent a result.

Use the procedure to construct the page plan, not to output an analysis or role-label checklist. Do not invent humor, surprise, a conclusion, new dialogue intent or an event absent from the source.
