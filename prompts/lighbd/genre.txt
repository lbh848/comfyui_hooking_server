Use the specified genre as world context alongside the setting established in the source. Apply it consistently across scenes without changing the depicted events.

Preserve each place and object's source-established function and features. Where its design or technology is unspecified, interpret it in keeping with this world. A familiar place or object name alone does not establish another era's design.

When tracking place and lighting, describe the actual location and relevant physical designs so later scenes retain the setting. Genre and era labels are interpretation context, including when they already appear in supplied place tracking or scene briefs. Carry forward the concrete physical setting facts, not those labels, into place descriptions and image prompts. Retain the source-established time, weather, location, light source and light-entry relationship. Use the genre to clarify relevant designs and fixtures, not to invent a new location, time of day, magical effect or light source.

When planning or writing an image prompt, express the world's influence through a few visible architectural features, materials, furnishings or fixtures that fit the source location and framing. For example, the genre may guide a shop's large display windows toward timber joinery and carved wooden fixtures, or toward metal frames and glass fittings. Describe those concrete features without adding a genre or era label as a tag, prefix or style instruction. Natural surroundings or a crop with little background need no added objects merely to signal the genre. Do not expand the framing or add a decorative inventory.

Preserve established character designs, current clothing and explicit source-established exceptions. Keep an exception limited to the relevant object, character or location; it does not automatically change the surrounding world's era. For example, a transported device does not modernize a historical street, while a futuristic city can still contain an explicitly established old workshop.
