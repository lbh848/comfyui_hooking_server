SCENE SPACE AND PHYSICAL LIGHTING

- Resolve the depicted space and the illumination actually reaching it from the original source, continuing context and supplied time/place/lighting state at each panel's instant. Preserve the established light source, entry path and brightness until an actual source-grounded change. A new crop, reaction, page or omitted lighting field does not change the room's illumination. When tracking is absent, recover these facts from the surrounding source; absence of tracking does not mean darkness.
- Separate emotional atmosphere, thoughts and figurative narration from observable conditions. A dark mood, cold stare, frozen atmosphere or heavy silence is not evidence of a dark room, cold-colored light, frost, fog, dramatic shadows or reduced exposure. Convey supported emotion through the owning person's brows, gaze, mouth, gesture and posture, without changing the environment or adding visual effects. Preserve genuinely established darkness and physical light changes; do not brighten every scene by default.
- Source-established physical facts outrank mood words in scene_brief or planning prose. A daylight-lit interior remains lit by that daylight during a hostile or sorrowful reaction, even when its window is off-frame. Darkness confined to an enclosed space does not spread into the surrounding room; a screen's local light does not erase the room's existing illumination. Do not invent cinematic lighting or color grading to match the mood.
- Keep page.caption limited to shared physical location, time, illumination and visible setting features. Keep page.instruction about page composition and reading flow. Put literal visible reactions in their owning actor instructions; do not put abstract mood labels into page, panel, object or actor image descriptions, and never convert emotional atmosphere into unsupported illumination or visual effects.

PAGE AND SHOTS

- Use 3-5 PRIMARY shots in reading and chronological order (never 2 panels). Represent each shot in panels, either as a substantial page division or as a borderless main scene with readable framed scenes layered over it. Choose 3, 4 or 5 supported shots to fit the passage without setting frequency quotas across pages or merging meaningful source moments merely to reduce the number of actor descriptions, dialogue boxes or coordinates you must write.
- Recover the source's visible preparation, action/contact changes, consequential detail and owning character's response. Give a supported change or revealing detail its own shot when it adds information or timing; several such moments must not collapse into one generic action panel followed by a generic reaction. Choose 3, 4 or 5 shots directly to preserve these moments and their reading rhythm without inventing events. Sentence count and number of speakers do not determine panel count. A static or unfinished passage stays static or unfinished.
- Plan panel count, relative areas and page size together. Give each primary panel enough room for its subject, causal cues and dialogue. A dominant shot must leave the other chosen views readable; its share of the page is a composition choice, not a fixed target. Choose a suitable offered page size and shot scale for the sequence.
- Choose between ordinary page divisions, one large borderless scene with smaller framed shots over its continuous background, or a combination of grouped divisions and overlays. In a layered page, the main scene counts as ONE shot and each distinct readable inset counts as ONE; do not add decorative thumbnails to reach the count. The smaller shots may be contained within the main scene's full-canvas bbox and must retain enough room for their action and dialogue.
- Distinguish the two families by actual geometry: a layered page has a full-canvas borderless scene with smaller scenes superimposed on it. A divided page with angled frames or a body crossing a divider is still divided. Preserve supported action, dialogue and readable chronology within either family.
- Select page hierarchy, flow and boundaries from the supplied vocabulary. For ordinary divisions, default boundaries to touching: adjacent artwork meets directly at a single black dividing stroke; do not outline each touching panel separately. For a borderless main scene with floating framed shots, use hierarchy dominant_panel or asymmetric, flow layered, and boundaries overlapping; use mixed when also combining locally divided groups. Use separated only for a deliberate pause. Do not apply touching/shared-divider instructions to the borderless background or its overlaid insets.
- page.instruction describes relative sizes, positions, overlap order and reading flow once (budget: 60 + 50 characters per panel). Refer to ordinary positions or visual roles, not panel IDs or enum names. page.caption adds only shared location, time, light direction and 2-4 visible setting features (about 200 characters); the server supplies boundary wording and fixed manga style metadata.
- Each panel has a unique semantic panelId for geometry connections; array order determines reading order. Select shape, camera_distance, camera_angle and focus from the vocabulary; instruction adds shot placement, crop, emphasis and useful spatial relations (about 140 characters, up to 180 when needed). The server expands the enums, so do not repeat them or dialogue here.
- Only an explicit request for a splash/full-page single image permits one shot. A multi-shot layered page needs no such request: represent its main scene as a panel with shape borderless and a full-canvas bbox, and each distinct smaller shot as a panel with shape inset, its own actors and dialogue, and a tight scene bbox. Keep attachedTo empty for floating insets and the background; containment or visual overlap is not a shared edge. Keep the panels array in reading order even when the main scene is read last; page.instruction specifies that it is the background layer. Do not add duplicate frame objects or draw panel names, numbers, INSET labels or layout IDs.
- Keep subjects large enough to read. Vary close-ups, medium framing, viewpoint and foreground scale rather than repeatedly cropping every body at a panel edge. Do not use full body, wide shot, very wide shot, scenery, landscape, fisheye or out of frame tags.
- Give each panel a source-grounded focal point: an acting gesture, a visible detail that explains the event, or its owning character's reaction. Emphasize the strongest beat through relative panel area, tighter framing, angle or depth; contrast neighboring views instead of repeating the same eye-level crop and pose. Preserve enough visible context to read the action and its owner. Never invent a new event merely to vary the shots.

PAGE COMPOSITION KNOWLEDGE

- Distinguish the sequence of depicted moments, the arrangement of panels on the page, and the composition inside each panel. Chronological moments do not each require a full-width row. A camera change alters a shot; it does not change the page division. Design the page as a whole before filling it with actor descriptions.
- A page has a hierarchy of spatial groups. Divide a larger area horizontally or vertically, then subdivide its groups as needed: a row can contain several neighboring panels, a column can contain several successive panels, and these groupings can nest within each other. A local column is one part of a page, not a requirement that every panel belong to one page-wide column. Use the source's connected beats to decide which views belong together.
- Panel divisions need not form an equal grid. A panel may extend alongside several neighboring panels; adjacent groups can have different widths, heights and subdivision directions. Offsetting the divisions between rows or columns creates staggered arrangements while preserving the larger grouping. Combining these relationships gives many compositions at the same panel count; there is no short menu of required patterns.
- Also consider depth: a continuous borderless main scene can extend to the canvas edges and remain visible around several smaller framed moments. Its focal subject or interaction may occupy the upper, central, lower, left, right or diagonal area; choose this from the source action, gaze, crop and available dialogue space. It may be one figure, an interaction between participants or a source-supported detail, rather than always a standing portrait. Place readable insets below, above, along either side, in separated corner groups or on a stepped diagonal around that focus. Protect the main face, action and dialogue while letting the continuous setting remain visible between frames. Keep each inset's own moment and setting distinct from the underlying scene; earlier views must not inherit a later outfit or lighting state. Describe the background layer, inset groups and overlap order in page.instruction, and each shot's own action in its panel instruction.
- When choosing this layered composition, FIRST compose one complete illustration across the ENTIRE canvas: establish its continuous environment, large main figure, other visible participants and main dialogue. THEN place the smaller framed scenes ON TOP of that illustration, covering only selected areas. The main scene remains underneath every inset and reaches all four canvas edges; the insets do not cut holes in it or split it into rows. Keep its full-canvas bbox even if only parts remain visible. In page.instruction explicitly state the image-model order: "One continuous full-canvas scene forms the background; smaller framed scenes are superimposed on it." Follow with the main figure's position, the inset group positions and the reading path. Do not describe this choice as a page divided into N regions or as a large bottom panel. A separate ordinary divided page still follows the existing division rules.
- Separate layer order, focal position and narrative order. Background means BEHIND the inset pictures, not below them on the page. The full-canvas scene may be read first, between inset groups or last, according to its source instant; array order and dialogue preserve that sequence. A later moment does not automatically belong at the bottom, and a main character does not have to begin below a top row of insets. Pick the focal position (upper, central, side or diagonal) that fits the beats directly and, when hints are enabled, use its matching hint ID. Across pages, avoid repeating the same focal position and inset grouping when the source supports an equally readable alternative. Vary positions naturally; do not mechanically rotate positions or invent action merely for variety.
- Do not arrange the whole page as one horizontal row of three or four side-by-side panels (1 row x 3 columns or 1 row x 4 columns). Use multiple rows, nested groups or a spanning panel instead. This restriction also applies when numbered composition hints are disabled.
- Establish visual hierarchy through relative area, proportion, position and contrast. Give a source-grounded focal moment room to hold attention, and arrange supporting actions, details or reactions as readable groups around or before/after it. Balanced divisions can give related moments comparable weight; asymmetry can distinguish their roles. Neither equal sizes nor one enormous dominant image is obligatory.
- Match panel proportions to the visible material. A broad panel can connect participants or carry a lateral action; a tall panel can follow a vertical gesture or sustain a figure beside a sequence of smaller views; a compact panel can isolate a revealing detail or response. These are expressive uses, not mandatory shot assignments. Preserve enough width for horizontal dialogue and enough space for each shot's essential action.
- Compose rhythm through relationships between panels. Similar proportions and repeated divisions can establish a measured cadence; changing scale or division can mark a supported shift, interruption or emphasis. Several distinct views can unpack an action, while a larger view can give a moment room to register. Panel size alone does not specify elapsed time, and variety alone is not a reason to add an event or distort a quiet passage.
- Make the reading path agree with the groups. Shared boundaries, alignment and proximity should make the next view apparent. Keep a consistent horizontal reading direction, and let the reader finish a small group before moving to its neighboring group. A tall panel beside several shorter panels needs a clear entry and exit so chronology does not depend on jumping over an unread panel. Use source-supported gaze, movement and balloon order to reinforce that path without changing the depicted action.
- Choose frame contour independently of the page grouping, for BOTH ordinary divided panels and superimposed inset pictures. Mix rectangular cuts with slanted quadrilaterals, leaning parallelograms, tapered frames, clipped corners and asymmetric manga outlines when the action, gaze or reading path benefits. These are expressive comic cuts, not decorative regular hexagons, polygon tiles or badges. Use shape diagonal or irregular for angled ordinary panels; shape inset identifies a superimposed shot without requiring a rectangle. Describe its actual sloping edges and taper in instruction. The axis-aligned bbox encloses the frame; it does not define a rectangular visible border. For touching angled panels, describe the same shared diagonal edge from both sides, with one ink stroke and no unintended gap. Keep faces, contact details and horizontal dialogue inside the readable area. Vary contour and proportions without forcing every panel to tilt; a contour change does not make a repeated page grouping a new composition.
- A layered page may also use THREE DEPTH LEVELS: the continuous environment at the back, the smaller framed scenes in the middle, and one main character's continuous body in front of selected inset borders. The foreground body belongs to the main scene's instant and viewpoint; it is not another shot. State this back-to-front order in page.instruction and describe which hair, shoulder, arm or silhouette edge covers which inset border. Use cross_panel_actors with relation page_foreground_overlay, the full-canvas home panel first in spansPanels, then only the inset panels actually crossed. Keep the body bbox tight and omit its duplicate from the home panel's actors. Independent depictions of the same character INSIDE the inset pictures remain in those panels' actors. Keep their faces and dialogue readable and never splice them into the foreground body. Merely spanning the vertical range of several insets does not require an overlay; use it for visible foreground occlusion.
- Select and adapt these principles for the event rather than copying a previous page or repeating a named template. Prefer nested groups, unequal divisions or a spanning panel over stacking full-width bands when the beats connect. Choose the spatial relationships that make their connection, emphasis and reading rhythm clear. When numbered hints are active, also obey their single-use composition rule.
- Express the chosen groups, relative areas and reading path directly in page.instruction and the actual panel bboxes and attachedTo edges, rather than relying on shape labels or camera angles to imply composition.

FRAME BREAKS AND CAST

- Choose the page's source beats and visual emphasis first. When a dominant character's gesture benefits from one purposeful frame break, let hair, a shoulder, a hand or an arm extend across a shared border, with the divider hidden BEHIND that part. A frame break serves the chosen event and camera; do not reorganize the page around a decorative foreground body. Quiet or reaction-only pages may omit a break.
- Emit this ONE continuous foreground body in cross_panel_actors with name, spansPanels (at least two existing panelIds, its home panel first), relation, bbox and instruction. Keep the home panel's camera and body scale as a part extends over a neighboring region. Never join a face close-up to a separate medium shot as one body; different shots remain separate occurrences. Describe visible identity/outfit/pose, the crossing part and overlap order. Its bbox tightly encloses the visible BODY, not whole panels. Hide the divider behind the crossing part; keep faces and dialogue clear.
- For page_foreground_overlay, state the occlusion from BOTH sides: the body instruction says which silhouette part is in front, and each crossed inset instruction says that its artwork and border pass behind that part. Place the inset mostly outside the foreground silhouette with only a deliberate edge overlap; keep its important face, action and dialogue uncovered. The border stops at the body contour and resumes beyond it, rather than continuing over the clothing or turning the whole inset into a picture pasted on the character.
- Do not duplicate the continuous crossing body in its owning panels' actors. For breaks_border or spans_adjacent_panels, the linked panels share that body. For page_foreground_overlay, only the first linked panel owns the main body: the remaining linked inset pictures may retain separate shots of the same character at their own source instants. Describe those inset figures independently; never confuse them with fragments of the foreground body. visibleNames lists the roster actors visible in each panel, including a crossing body.
- Other actors receive one item per visible panel occurrence: name is the exact roster name; bbox encloses the visible body; instruction carries exactly one person-count tag matching the character's supplied gender_tag when present (such as 1girl, 1boy, 1male or 1other), visible identity/hair/face/eye tags, current wardrobe or coverage, expression, gaze, pose/action and crop. A cross-panel body needs the same complete visible description.
- Every actor occurrence is a complete description of ONLY its own visible body within this shot. Completeness means preserving all applicable visible settings, not copying the whole Profile into every crop. A name, another panel's instruction or a reference picture never supplies omitted visible tags; equally, Profile tags for wholly unseen regions must not summon those regions into view. In a hand-only shot, describe the visible hand and its action; omit head, eye and facial-expression tags when those regions are wholly outside the crop.

CHARACTER SETTINGS

- The selected Profile is authoritative for fixed identity. Read appearance, face_tags, eye_tags, eye_prompt and absolute_tags together; appearance is not the complete list. Combine the supplied settings rather than choosing one list as a replacement for another. default_outfit supplies the garment design when no current source/state change supersedes it. Use the supplied profile timeline for each panel's own source instant; the assigned anchor's Profile is not permission to apply a later transformation to earlier panels.
- Copy every visible identity tag verbatim into EACH owning actor instruction, including repeated occurrences and cross_panel_actors. Preserve hair length, base color, inner/streak colors and hairstyle together, as well as facial features, accessories and eye/pupil identity. Preserve exact symbolic spellings and weights. A generic pupil tag never replaces a supplied specific shape; a generic hair tag never replaces supplied color placement or style. face_tags and eye_tags add to appearance, never erase its more specific tags. Use eye_prompt as that character's eye-description constraint alongside those tags.
- Include the selected Profile's applicable absolute_tags verbatim in every instruction depicting that character. They are required positive tags, not optional context, and follow the same visibility, current-state and character_negative rules as other settings. Omit a tag only for its wholly unseen region or an explicit conflicting state/exclusion; preserve all remaining settings. Required tags never force an unseen body part into the crop. Keep each character's settings attached only to that character.
- Retain every supplied visible or coverage-defining garment detail: colors, trim, layers, fastenings, accessories and silhouette. A generic garment name is insufficient when its design tags are supplied. Source-supported changes determine which garments are worn and their condition, without redesigning the remaining garments. Do not infer replacement clothing from an activity alone. When tracking is absent, resolve changes from the source at the depicted instant and earlier context, using Profile defaults where the source is silent.
- Apply visibility to each trait's own region in the chosen shot: omit a trait when that region is wholly outside the crop or naturally fully occluded, or an explicit current state/exclusion supersedes it. Retain every visible trait even in a tight shot; another body part's focus, a reference picture, unfamiliar tag meaning and prompt compression never justify dropping a visible setting. A detail view that excludes the head must not carry facial or eye descriptions merely to establish identity. Choose expression and gaze only for visible facial regions and keep them compatible with fixed identity. Panel/page instruction length budgets do not limit applicable actor identity or wardrobe tags.

STATE AND VISIBLE ACTION

- First distinguish independent actions, directed interactions, mutual interactions, continuing states and visible responses in the source. They may coexist, but none establishes the others: a pose alone does not establish an action, an action does not establish a clothing change or reaction, and a held result does not depict the transfer that caused it. Resolve the action, participant roles, current state and visibility separately before choosing the shot. Keep these decisions internal and express them through the existing fields.
- Profile supplies identity; state.outfit/state.hair override defaults for their slot. Without state, resolve from source chronology and earlier context, then fall back to Profile. Missing state never means no clothes. Keep garment design/colors stable; source-supported wetness, damage, opening or removal changes condition, not outfit identity. Anchor state is the END state: do not copy it backward into earlier panels. Preserve a continuing state only while it remains established; never restore an ended state merely because it appears in earlier context.
- Depict one coherent instant per panel. Preserve actor/receiver, connected anatomy, support, contact direction and causal cues. Earlier events explain only facts still true; never invent an event, result, joke or speech. Match emotional intensity with that actor's brows, gaze, mouth and posture.
- Bind every directed interaction to its acting participant and receiving participant or object from the source; never infer direction from gender, roster order or screen position. A mutual interaction must preserve both participants' supported contributions rather than becoming two unrelated solo actions. Several interactions may share a participant only when their contact, support and posture can coexist at this instant.
